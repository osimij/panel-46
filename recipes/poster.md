# Poster

For: a poster, a cover, an announcement or a social picture: one picture that has to work at a glance.

## What makes it great

- **One idea.** A poster is seen for two seconds. It says one thing, and the picture and the words say it together, not twice.
- **An order for the eye.** One thing it lands on first, then a second, then the small print. Never three things fighting.
- **It works small and rewards coming close.** Readable as a thumbnail or from across a street, with something more to find up close.
- **Type is the picture, or serves it.** Never a title laid over an image that didn't need it.
- **Something made, not assembled.** A real object, a drawing with a hand in it, a strange crop, a material. A stock picture with a title on it is the template.

If the name on it could be swapped for another event's and nothing else would need to change, it is not done.

## Before the first step

Read the project card, the canvas (get_project) and get_verdicts. Then ask only what they don't already answer, at most four questions, in one message:

1. What is it for, and what must someone know after two seconds?
2. Where will it be seen: printed (what size), on a screen, on Instagram (which shape)?
3. What must be on it (date, place, logos, sponsors), and in which language?
4. How should it feel? Find five or six saves in their library that feel different from each other, show them in a row, and ask which are closest and which are wrong.

Write what you learned as a note on the canvas (add_note), in their words. Then start_steps.

Every sketch of a poster is one board in the poster's shape, in the middle of the page, as large as fits the screen. Size everything inside it by the board's width (container units), so it looks the same at any size.

## Steps

### Message

What the poster says, before how it looks.

- Read the designer's writing skill first (get_skill: the one that leads "writing").
- The one line: what someone must take away in two seconds. Offer three, from different sides, in the poster's language.
- Then the facts that must be on it, in order of importance. Three levels at most: read first, read second, small print. Anything that is none of the three comes off.
- Real names, dates and places from the project. Never invented ones.

Ask which line, and whether the order of the facts is right. Then: go on to the idea?

Decision to write down: the line chosen, and the three levels.

### Idea

The poster's gem: one visual idea that says the line without repeating it.

- Look for it in what is true about the event, the place or the people. Read the designer's rules again: something physical made fully real is one way, and half-real is the failure.
- Search the library for how others did it (search_library with look), and read get_verdicts.
- Offer three ideas, different in kind: type alone as the picture; one object made real; an illustration or a pattern; a photograph treated one way. For each: one line saying what it is, one line saying why it fits this and nothing else, and two or three saves that show the craft. Place those saves under the topic Idea.
- Test each: with another event's name on it, would it still work? Then it is decoration. Drop it.
- When they pick one, make a study of it: the idea alone, large, with real craft and knobs for what is worth tuning (and a seed knob if anything is random). If it is a picture to reuse, the designer can point at it and press Keep to put it in the project's brand assets.

After the three ideas, ask which one. After the study, ask: go on to the layout, or keep working on the idea?

Decision to write down: the idea in one sentence.

### Layout

Where things go, in gray, at the poster's real shape.

- A wireframe (make_sketch with kind "wireframe"), "Layout": the board in the poster's shape, with gray blocks for the idea, the line and each level of facts. No colors, no chosen fonts.
- Show the order for the eye: what is biggest, what is second, where the small print sits. Say it in a line.
- Offer two or three layouts side by side in the one sketch when there is a real choice (the idea filling the board with the words small; the words huge with the idea inside them).
- Look at it small, the size of a thumbnail. The first thing must still read.

Ask which layout, or what to change. Then: go on to Color?

Decision to write down: the layout in a line, with the order for the eye.

### Color

Topic: color

- If the project has brand colors, start from them.
- Few colors. One that carries the poster, and what it sits on. Say why, from the idea.
- Your own colors, not one of Panel's palettes, unless the designer asks for a palette.
- Show the layout with the colors and the idea's study in place. When there is a real choice, show two, each as its own sketch. Colors are knobs, each named by what it is for.
- If it will be printed, say which colors may come out duller on paper.

Ask which one, or what to change. Then: go on to Typography?

Decision to write down: each color as hex with where it goes.

### Typography

Topic: typography

- The designer's own fonts first (get_fonts).
- Judge every font in the poster's real words, in its language, at the size it will be. Never by a Latin specimen.
- One family is often enough. Two at most.
- The line is set with care: where it breaks, how tight, how it sits against the idea. Try it large enough to be the picture.

Show the type on the colored layout. Ask which, or what to change. Then: go on to the poster?

Decision to write down: the families, weights and sizes.

### Poster

The real thing.

- One sketch, "Poster", from the topic Poster: the idea at full craft, the line, the facts, the colors and the type, on the board in its real shape.
- Look at it small (a thumbnail) and at full size, and fix what breaks at either.
- The last check, before you show it. Answer these in your reply, honestly:
  1. What does someone take away in two seconds?
  2. What is the one thing they will remember? If you can't name it, it isn't done: go back to it.
  3. With another event's name on it, would it still work? Where?
  4. Which of the designer's rules does it brush against, and why is each of those still there?

There is no decision to lock. The designer chooses the sketch (Choose).
